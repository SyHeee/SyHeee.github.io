At the front of the lecture hall, Professor Miller pointed his chalk at a butterfly-shaped graph on the board. Freya copied it into her notebook. It came out looking more like a pretzel.

Tuesday, week two. Math 123, Ordinary Differential Equations, was already making her nervous.

"Imagine running the same simulation twice, using exactly the same equations. The only difference is that we change one of the starting values by a millionth. At first, the two paths look almost identical. But over time, the two paths move further and further apart."

"That's why it's so difficult to predict the weather two weeks from now. Even a tiny error in measuring today's weather can lead to a very different forecast."

He paused and pointed to the two wings of the butterfly.

"But here's the interesting part. Although we can't predict exactly where each path will go in the long run, both paths tend to stay within the same general shape. This is what we call the Lorenz attractor."

"The future may be unpredictable, but that doesn't mean it has no structure."

He smiled and turned back to his laptop.

"Something to think about. The first problem set is already on the course website. It's due Friday."

Friday was also the last day Freya could drop the class. She decided to finish the assignment before then. If she couldn't get through the first problem set, she wasn't sure she should stay in the course.

A hand shot up in the second row. The boy wore a Patagonia vest, had a fresh haircut, and held an Apple Pencil over his iPad as if every word the professor said might be on the exam.

"Professor, if a tiny typo in our code changes the final answer on Question 3, do we lose points for landing in the wrong spot? Or do we still get partial credit as long as we stay inside the butterfly?"

Several students groaned quietly. Before Miller could answer, a voice came from three rows behind Freya. The voice sounded calm, almost bored.

"Bro, if your code actually escapes the attractor, you've got bigger problems than your homework. Submit it to *Nature* instead of Gradescope."

A few students laughed. Professor Miller smiled and leaned against the desk.

"He has a point. I'm not expecting everyone to get exactly the same trajectory. Just make sure your method is correct and show your work."

Freya drew a small star next to *Question 3* in her notebook, then glanced over her shoulder, but couldn't tell who had spoken.

By now, Freya had already divided everyone in Evans Hall 207 into three statistical categories.

First, there were the boys in old hackathon hoodies who looked like they hadn't washed their hair since the semester began. They could probably calculate a Lyapunov exponent in their sleep, which made them ideal partners for problem sets.

Second, there were the economics students in Patagonia vests. Most sat near the exit, though the boy in the second row was an exception. They took notes as seriously as if they were preparing for a meeting with investors.

And third, sitting somewhere in the middle-left rows, were girls like Freya: neat ponytails, color-coded Muji pens, and the constant anxiety of being exactly 1.5 standard deviations above the mean.

Smart enough to consider a double major in Math and Computer Science. Still lying awake every other night, wondering if it was a good idea.

After class, Freya went to Kimchi Yard, the Korean restaurant she and her roommate Lisa often visited. Lisa was already at their usual table in the corner, waving at Freya with one hand while protecting her hot bowl of bibimbap with the other.

"Freya, you won't believe who I just ran into outside the library this afternoon," Lisa said before Freya had even put her bag down. "Emily. In a blazer. At two in the afternoon."

She stirred a big spoonful of gochujang into her rice.

"She's applying to business school now. Some guy at the career fair told her quant traders make four hundred grand right out of college." Lisa rolled her eyes. "I wanted to tell her, girl, you were crying over derivatives last week. Who are you trying to fool? Half these future quant traders found out about the job on TikTok. The salary came first. The math can catch up later."

Freya laughed. But she also reminded herself to be careful. Anything she told Lisa usually made it around the whole floor by the end of the week.

Lisa slid a second bowl across the table. "I ordered extra. You look like you've been living on granola bars."

"I have."

"I know. I saw the wrappers in your trash."

That was Lisa. She didn't read textbooks. She read people.

Last semester, before the Stats 134 final exam, Freya did all forty practice problems twice, color-coded her notes, and slept four hours. Lisa went to office hours once and came back with a theory.

"He's putting a Markov chain question on it," she said. "He touched his glasses three times when he talked about them."

She studied that one topic, watched a review video at double speed, and was asleep by eleven.

There was a Markov chain question on the exam. It was worth thirty points.

Freya got a 94. Lisa got an 81.

Freya had actually done the math. As far as she could tell, Lisa had spent about half as much time studying and scored eighty-six percent as high as she did. That gave Lisa a relative efficiency of 1.72, compared to Freya's 1.0. She had checked twice.

Statistically speaking, Freya was the one doing something wrong.

Freya picked up her spoon. The rice was perfect, of course. She had been living on coffee and granola bars for the past three days.

"Have you declared your major yet?" Freya asked. "Aren't you getting tired of putting it off?"

"God, no. Why would I do that to myself?" Lisa took a bite of rice and immediately regretted it. Too hot.

"I'm waiting until next semester. Let everyone else sacrifice their GPAs in those introductory classes. I'll take two easy breadth courses, figure out which departments have decent professors, and make my decision when everyone else is done suffering."

Freya stared at her for a second, then slowly set her spoon down.

They were sitting at the same table, paying the exact same tuition, and living under the same roof. Yet somehow, Freya felt like she was solving an optimization problem with a hundred constraints, while Lisa seemed to have found the answer without even reading the question.

Half an hour later, they were walking back to their apartment. The evening fog was coming in from the bay, and the streetlights had just come on. Lisa swung her tote bag as she moved on from academic planning to a subject she found much more interesting: other people's lives.

"By the way," Lisa said, looking at Freya, "are we going to talk about Daniel from your linear algebra section last semester, or are you pretending you haven't noticed?"

"Noticed what?" Freya was still thinking about Professor Miller's butterfly. "He asked me for lecture notes last semester because he overslept."

"Freya, please. Nobody accidentally sits at the same table as you in the engineering library three nights in a row just to get a PDF. And he's still doing it this semester." Lisa nudged her with her elbow. "He's cute, he's pre-med, and he's clearly interested in you. How have you not noticed?"

Freya adjusted the strap of her backpack. Daniel *was* nice. He was polite, organized, and always replied to messages. On paper, there was nothing wrong with him.

"He was probably just trying to pass the exam."

Lisa looked at her for a second and gave up.

"Okay, forget Daniel. What about Benson? You guys talked for forty minutes on Sunday. Are you finally going to visit him in LA over spring break? Or are you two going to keep calling yourselves childhood friends until graduation?"

Freya was beginning to lose her patience. It wasn't Lisa's fault. Between her unfinished double-major declaration form and Problem Set 1, she already had enough decisions to make.

Her brain was already at full capacity. Whatever was going on with Daniel or Benson would have to wait.

"Benson is in his first year of a PhD program, trying to survive his AI research, and I'm trying to figure out whether I should drop a class before five on Friday," Freya said, sounding more annoyed than she had intended. "We're just friends, Lisa. Can we please not talk about this tonight?"

"Alright, alright, message received." Lisa raised both hands, though a small, skeptical smirk lingered at the corner of her mouth. "But you know, one of these days, you're going to spend so much time trying to figure out your life that you'll forget to actually live it."

Freya let out a quiet breath and didn't answer.

By the time they reached their building's front steps, she was feeling a little calmer. She looked up at the streetlamp. A small moth flew around it in uneven circles, sometimes moving closer to the light, sometimes further away. No two circles seemed quite the same, yet the moth always stayed near the light.
